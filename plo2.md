In my class, Experimental Psychology, we learned the basics of research design in psychology and performed our own research as a class. I completed a literature review, drafted hypotheses, conducted research, interpreted data, and wrote a formal research paper. Completed in August of 2026, my paper, titled, Gratitude and Prosocial Intentions: The Moderating Effect of Social Desirability, taught me how to write a professional research paper from start to finish. I learned how to pick a research design, recruit participants, chart data, interpret data analysis and write it so that it is logical to readers. 

This project was significant to me because it encouraged me to do something I had never done before. It challenged me to learn how to write a professional paper while adhering to the same steps the rest of my classmates used. It taught me to let go of perfectionism and be content with the work of my teammates. I learned that I did not have to get every detail of the paper perfect to still abide by the style guide. Just because I did not watch my classmates conduct the experiment on their participants did not mean that they did not do it correctly. I learned to trust that they were pulling their weight. 

We examined the moderating effect of social desirability on gratitude and prosocial intentions. We conducted a 2x2 between-subjects design with a small sample size (N = 67). We hypothesized that the effect of gratitude would significantly depend on whether the participant had a high or low level of social desirability. We also aimed to show that social desirability and gratitude would increase prosocial intentions. This was measured through the use of the Marlowe-Crowne Social Desirability Scale (measuring social desirability), a thought activity (measuring the gratitude variable), and a hypothetical donation scenario (measuring outcome). Results were computed using a 2x2 factorial Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) test. We found that social desirability has a significant effect on prosocial intentions (F(1, 63) = 6.83, p = .011, η² = .092) while the gratitude condition did not have a significant effect on prosocial intentions (F(1, 63) = 3.74, p =.058, η² = .050). There was no significant interaction between the variables (F(1, 63) = 0.63, p = .429). This may be because of a small sample size and a lack of control for the religious orientation of the participants. 

While completing this project, I learned how little I knew about research design. As I started that class, I thought I had a decent understanding because I was so close to the end of my degree. However, I quickly learned that the old adage, “you don’t know what you don’t know” is still very true. I had to learn how to decide which research method to use, what variables would accurately test our hypothesis, and to accurately interpret data analysis. The project challenged me to focus on why I was completing the work rather than the struggle of learning the nuances. Ultimately, I was completing the work because it was developing my skills. That perspective shift helped me come out of the experience with a positive view of research and reminded me why it is so important. This project also taught me the importance of having a scientific mind. 

A scientific mind looks at the world through the lens of curiosity. It uses empirical evidence and logical reasoning to develop a picture of the world. The scientific mind is also humble and willing to admit to mistakes and adapt to new information. Because my hypothesis was unsupported, I had the opportunity to adapt in real time. Experimental psychology grew my scientific mind and I began to look at the events of my life and wonder why they happened the way they did. I wondered why people made the choices they made and what influenced them. Completing an entire research project opened my curiosity to a new level. Increased curiosity and a continual reduction of perfectionism will serve me well as I continue on this journey. 

The skills I gained while completing this project and PLO have greatly prepared me for OT school. They have taught me the basics of research methods, professional style APA papers, and data analysis and interpretation. This knowledge will serve me well as I further my education and eventually become a practicing OT. The skills of performing and understanding research are crucial to ensure that interventions are backed by research. I have seen this to be true in my work with young adults and have seen that interventions are most helpful when they have been well researched as effective. I look forward to continually learning as the field continues to develop.
